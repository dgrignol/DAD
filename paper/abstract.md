**Tracking dynamic representations of predictions, sensory evidence, and prediction error during moving-object perception with MEG**

Predictive coding accounts propose that perception depends on comparing incoming sensory evidence with expectations generated from prior knowledge and recent context. In many M/EEG paradigms, prediction error is studied by contrasting evoked responses to expected and unexpected static stimuli. This approach is powerful, but questions remain open about what is being represented over time and whether the neural activity reflects the expected stimulus, the observed stimulus, or the mismatch between them. Moving-object occlusion provides a test case for this questions. When an object disappears, its recent trajectory can be used to predict where it should be; when it reappears, this prediction can either be confirmed or violated.

We recorded MEG while participants viewed a dot moving along smooth curved trajectories. The dot was either always visible and predictable, temporary occluded but continued and reappeared along the same predictable path (occluded non-deviant), or changed direction while hidden, resulting in an unpredictable reappearance and continuation after occlusion (occluded deviant). Using dynamic representational similarity analysis, we tracked how strongly time-resolved MEG activity reflected the observed trajectory, the expected continuation, and prediction-error models.

Preliminary analyses (N = 7) revealed reliable position-related structure during visible motion, with neural representations lagging the stimulus by approximately 120–130 ms. In always-visible trials, position information was strongest early in the trajectory and weakened later, despite continuous visual input. In non-deviant occluded trials, position representations were strong before occlusion, attenuated while the dot was hidden, and increased again after reappearance, consistent with reinstatement of the expected trajectory when sensory evidence matched prediction. In deviant trials, after reappearance, MEG activity aligned more strongly with the observed deviated path than with the expected continuation, suggesting rapid updating when prediction and sensory evidence diverged. Prediction-error models showed weaker and less focal structure than observed-position models, providing tentative evidence that the current signal is dominated by representational updating rather than a separable prediction-error code.

These preliminary results suggest that position representations are weakened or transformed during occlusion rather than maintained as precise spatial predictions. More broadly, they show how dynamic RSA can move beyond expected-versus-unexpected evoked-response contrasts by tracking whether neural representations follow predictions, sensory evidence, or their mismatch over time.
